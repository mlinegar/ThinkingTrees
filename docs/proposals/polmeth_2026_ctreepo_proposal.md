# C-TreePO: Design-Based Partial Document Supervision for Long-Document Political Measurement

Category: Theory or Methods

Long political texts are increasingly measured through LLM pipelines, but once those texts exceed practical context limits the main methodological problem is no longer only prediction error. It is design. The analyst typically observes a truncated excerpt, a retrieved subset of passages, or a recursively summarized representation rather than the original document itself. Design-based Supervised Learning (DSL), especially Egami et al. (2024), shows why this matters: replacing gold-standard labels with cheaper surrogates can bias downstream inference even when the surrogate looks accurate on headline metrics. In long-document settings there is an additional source of nonclassical measurement error that DSL alone does not settle. The measurement target may already have been changed by the compression pipeline before any final label is assigned. This paper argues that long-document political measurement therefore requires a design-based account of compression, not just a design-based account of prediction.

That gap is increasingly important in political methodology. Recent work on political text, including Benoit et al. (2025), demonstrates that LLMs can recover useful measurements at scale, including ideological positions from party manifestos and other high-value coding tasks that once required expensive expert labor. At the same time, DSL makes clear that social scientists should not treat cheap machine-generated labels as plug-in replacements for gold-standard data. Existing work leaves a missing middle. The surrogate in practice is often not just a predicted label. It is a compressed representation of the original text: a summary, a span selection, a tree of summaries, or a retrieved context assembled for later scoring. If that representation drops interaction-bearing information, then downstream debiasing acts on a distorted object. The result is a political measurement pipeline in which preprocessing decisions become part of the inferential design, even though they are rarely treated that way.

C-TreePO addresses this problem by treating long-document coding as an oracle-preserving compression problem. The key object is an oracle: the full-information judgment the researcher actually wants and would trust if cost and context were not binding. In the running application here, the oracle is the Manifesto Project RILE judgment a trusted coder, or a high-quality rubric-governed coding procedure, would assign after reading the full manifesto. C-TreePO builds a compression tree over contiguous document spans, stores bounded summaries at the leaves and internal nodes, and audits whether the resulting compressed object preserves the oracle-relevant signal. The framework does not claim generic lossless compression. Its narrower and more useful claim is task-relative: a compressed representation is valid if it preserves the distinctions the oracle uses.

The method is built around three local laws stated in applied rather than symbolic terms. First, leaf summaries must retain the evidence that matters for the task rather than merely sounding plausible in isolation. Second, re-summarizing an already valid summary should not move the target; cleanup passes should be stable rather than silently rewriting the ideological signal. Third, merged summaries should agree with what a direct read of the combined span would imply; cross-span interactions cannot be lost when two children are combined into a parent. In the paper these conditions are called sufficiency, idempotence, and merge consistency. Their value for political methodology is that they convert an opaque summarization workflow into an auditable design. Instead of trusting recursive summarization because it is convenient, the analyst can estimate where and how often it changes the target quantity.

The design-based contribution follows directly from that setup. C-TreePO uses a supervision design that combines full-document labels with sampled substructure labels. The document-level target remains anchored to the full-document oracle. Additional supervision is then collected only on sampled leaves, spans, or internal nodes, with logged propensities for the sampled substructure population. This makes partial document supervision explicit rather than ad hoc. Once those propensities are logged, node-level discrepancies can be estimated with IPW or DSL-style corrections instead of being trusted naively. The strong methodological claim of the paper is therefore not simply that a tree of summaries can work in practice. It is that C-TreePO extends the design-based logic of DSL to long-document settings where the surrogate is a compressed representation, not just a predicted label.

Manifesto Project RILE scoring is an especially useful application for this argument. It is central to comparative politics, it has an established coding tradition, and it is exactly the kind of task where naive compression can fail. Manifestos are long, internally structured documents whose ideological content depends on how issue emphases combine across sections. A party may sound left on welfare policy, centrist on institutional reform, and right on law and order or market organization. A local summary can be individually reasonable and still distort the global RILE signal if those interactions disappear during merging. That makes RILE an ideal running case for a theory-and-methods paper about oracle-preserving measurement. The empirical setting in the repo already reflects the right supervision structure: document-level labels at the root, plus sampled node-level supervision with logged propensities for the internal population. The available corpus is also large enough to motivate the problem concretely without turning the paper into a benchmark exercise: local inspection identified 3,327 text-available manifesto documents with RILE labels from 62 countries between 1945 and 2025, including 1,009 texts longer than 8,192 characters. The paper will emphasize that long-document subset, where compression is a real design constraint rather than a convenience.

The broader payoff is a political-methods contribution that extends well beyond manifestos while remaining text-centric. The same design problem appears in legislative speeches, parliamentary debates, hearing transcripts, party platforms, court opinions, and other long political documents that are increasingly analyzed with LLM-based measurement pipelines. In each case, researchers face the same temptation to treat summarization, retrieval, or chunk selection as a neutral preprocessing step. C-TreePO argues that these choices are part of the measurement design itself. They determine what information survives to the final coding stage, and they should therefore be audited, sampled, and corrected with the same care political methodologists now bring to surrogate-label inference.

The paper’s contribution to PolMeth is thus a bridge between two active methodological conversations. One conversation asks how to use LLMs to produce valid political measurements at scale. The other, crystallized by DSL, asks how to preserve inferential validity when learned surrogates replace direct human coding. C-TreePO connects them by showing that long-document pipelines require a third object of analysis: the compressed representation on which the surrogate label is built. By making that representation auditable and by tying partial document supervision to logged, design-based correction, the paper offers a method for estimating or certifying document-level ideological signal without assuming that a full-document read is available everywhere. That is the sense in which C-TreePO is a political methodology contribution rather than a systems pitch: it recasts long-document LLM measurement as a principled design problem and supplies a design-based answer.
