\section{Introduction}\label{sec:min-introduction}

Two facts are well known. First, LLMs carry their own (sometimes
substantial) biases on text of any length. Second, even small biases in
LLM-produced labels translate into substantial biases in downstream
regression estimates, even when label accuracy looks high
\citep{EgamiEtAl2024}. Together they raise the bar for using LLMs as
measurement tools in empirical research: high-accuracy LLM scoring on a
large corpus does not guarantee unbiased downstream regression
coefficients, and the size of the resulting bias is not bounded by the
size of the labeling error. Long documents amplify the problem. LLMs
degrade on information buried in the middle of long inputs
\citep{LiuEtAl2024LostMiddle}, and a single long-context read on a
hundred-page document gives no traceable account of which
rubric-relevant distinctions the model recovered and which it missed.

Correcting for the bias requires a trusted gold-standard label to
compare the LLM against. Gold-standard labels for long documents are
expensive: each one needs an expert to read the full document under a
rubric, and reading time grows roughly linearly in document length.
The Manifesto Project illustrates the scale: trained coders score
party platforms (often $50$--$200$ pages each) along six policy
dimensions; the released dataset is $2{,}157$ platforms accumulated
over decades, and the marginal cost of an additional platform is the
same as the first. A researcher who wants labels at the rate new
documents arrive cannot scale out of that cost.

Our key assumption is that the oracle judgment of a long document
decomposes across the leaves and merges of a recursive summarization
tree. Concretely, we split a document into contiguous leaves
(uniform chunks of fixed size, paragraph boundaries, section breaks, or
any other contiguous segmentation), summarize each leaf, merge sibling
summaries pairwise up a tree, and score the root; we want the root
score to equal what a trusted coder would have assigned to the full
document. Three local laws make the assumption precise: each leaf
summary preserves the evidence its leaf carries (C1), re-summarizing a
stored summary does not drift (C2), and split-then-merge preserves the
same cross-leaf interactions as summarize-together (C3). Under those laws, two consequences follow
(Section~\ref{sec:min-guarantees}): the root summary's task value
equals the full document's (\emph{root preservation}), and any two
merge schedules satisfying the same realized laws give the same root
value (\emph{schedule invariance}). With an additional
oracle-factorization assumption, the laws also imply that algorithms
such as DPO and GRPO trained on root summaries target the same
population minimum as on full documents.

The frame is an approximate sufficient statistic on a tree merge:
$f(g(g(u)\concat g(v))) \approx f(u\concat v)$, with $g$ the
summarizer and $f$ the readout. Fisher and Blackwell give the static
form \citep{Fisher1922,Blackwell1953}. Mergeable summaries
\citep{Agarwal2013MergeableTODS,FlajoletEtAl2007,CormodeMuthukrishnan2005}
add an associative merge axiom, with sufficient statistics for a fixed
catalog of queries (Count-Min for item frequency, HyperLogLog for
cardinality, GK for quantiles). Sketch-Flip-Merge extends mergeable
summaries to local differential privacy, where sufficiency must
survive a noisy local randomizer between leaf and merge
\citep{HehirTingCormode2023SFM}. In each of these generations, $(f,
g)$ was hand-crafted: someone wrote down register-wise max,
hash-and-min, or flip-and-merge so the sufficiency proof would go
through analytically on the fixed query catalog.

C-TreePO replaces the hand-crafting with optimization in a function
space. $(f, g)$ live in a parameterized class; the local laws are the
axioms the joint solution must satisfy; the audit estimates residual
law violations on the deployed tree. Two regimes cover the
practical instantiations. For LLM-shaped $g$, the optimization is
prompt or weight tuning against the oracle, and the genuinely new
ingredient is observability of the summary itself: $g(x)$ is text, so
sufficiency becomes empirically auditable by direct reading and
counterfactual fiber sampling, alongside the algebraic checks
classical sketches rely on. For continuous structured inputs without
an LLM as the right vehicle, $g$ is a neural operator, a learned
mapping between function spaces \citep{Kovachki23NeuralOperator,Li21FNO}.
The local-law machinery is the same in either regime, and the local
laws are equivalent to the mergeable-summary axioms on the realized
state space (Theorem~\ref{thm:sketch-equivalence}), so the classical
and learned regimes inherit each other's guarantees.

The local laws have two equivalent uses. At audit time, the audit
estimates how often each law holds on a deployed tree from sampled
gold-standard labels. At training time, adding a $\lambda$-weighted
local-law penalty to the oracle-fit objective projects the learned
operator toward the subspace where the laws hold
(Section~\ref{sec:min-projection-estimation}). The audit machinery: treat each
realized leaf, stored summary, and merge node as a finite-population
unit; sample with logged inclusion probabilities; reweight by inverse
probability (Horvitz--Thompson) to estimate violation rates and oracle
distortion. This is design-based supervised
learning applied at the compression step itself. When the laws hold the
algebra preserves the root; when they fail the audit reports the size
of the failure, and the gap-bound theorem
(Section~\ref{sec:min-guarantees}) converts that into a finite-sample
envelope on the downstream estimand.

The pair lets a researcher reallocate gold-standard supervision. The
audit's estimation envelope responds to the total count of labeled
units, full-document or sampled-node alike. Node labels are often much
cheaper than document labels (span-level codings, leaf-level scores
from a deliberation-grade LLM, or the Manifesto Project's own
quasi-sentence annotations, of which the corpus has $\sim\!2.27$M),
and the audit certifies their use without bias. Every scored leaf or
merge then counts toward the same shrinking envelope as one
gold-standard read of the full document, so the choice of where to
spend the next gold label is no longer pinned to the document level.
The object of supervision becomes a design choice: the researcher
picks the unit of measurement that gives the most signal per unit of
expert effort, and the audit machinery makes the choice defensible.

On the Manifesto Project six-dimension benchmark of
\citet{BenoitEtAl2025}, a single open-weight C-tree pipeline equals or
outperforms their frontier proprietary 18-score ensemble across six
policy dimensions.
The structural headline is leaf-size invariance. A researcher can split a
manifesto into $\sim\!1\mathrm{K}$-token leaves or
$\sim\!16\mathrm{K}$-token leaves (a $16\times$ range) and the macro
correlation against expert means stays in a narrow band; the
alternating prompt ladder shows the same plateau on the economic
dimension across $1024$--$8192$ token leaves. Schedule invariance
(Cor.~\ref{cor:schedule}) is the formal version with leaves held fixed;
Section~\ref{sec:min-manifesto-results} reports the empirical extension
across leaf partitions, where the choice of how finely to split a
document becomes an audit-budget question (smaller leaves give more
sampling sites) and not a question of what is being measured.
