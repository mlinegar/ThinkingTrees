\begin{abstract}
Preference optimization on long documents is hard because hierarchical summarization can destroy the task signal before the optimizer ever sees the document.
We introduce \emph{Compression Tree Preference Optimization} (C-TreePO), which treats recursive summarization as a learned mergeable sketch: split a document into contiguous blocks, summarize locally, and audit whether each merge preserves the task oracle.
The framework is organized around three local laws: each leaf summary must preserve the evidence the task depends on, re-summarizing a stored summary must not change that evidence, and split-then-merge must preserve the same cross-span interactions as summarize-together.
Our main results form a ladder: local validity yields root-level oracle preservation and schedule invariance, adding downstream factorization yields the same population target as full-document DPO/GRPO training, and adding calibration and sampling assumptions turns sampled audits into an empirical preference-gap certificate. The training and audit paths read as two sides of one formal equivalence: supervising with local-law weights projects the learned summarizer onto the subspace where the laws hold, and auditing with node samples is the finite-sample estimator of the structural assumption that the approximation error is zero exactly on that subspace.
We demonstrate the mechanism on a changepoint-counting task whose oracle counts the number of hidden-register shifts in a sequence of scenes---the synthetic analogue of a literary-mood arc---where decomposing the document into a tree of smaller subproblems matches or improves on a flat Fourier Neural Operator baseline across the full range of supervision budgets, including when only $10\%$ of documents receive root-level labels.
The sufficient statistic for this task is simple---a triple at each node---but the same structural mechanism extends to settings where the local summaries are high-dimensional learned representations, including semantic tasks such as manifesto RILE scoring.
\end{abstract}
